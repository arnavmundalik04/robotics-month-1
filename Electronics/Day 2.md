Voltage Dividers: The principle behind a voltage divider is straightforward: input voltage is distributed across the resistors in proportion to their resistance values. The voltage drop across each resistor is a fraction of the total input voltage, and this fraction or ratio is directly related to the resistor's value relative to the total resistance in the circuit. These offer voltage measurement with precision. The voltage drops across a circuit are proportional to the resistance values. 
VX = (RX/RTotal) x VApplied        
Another way to represent this is,
VOUT = VIN x R2 / (R1+R2) 
•	If R1 is much larger than R2, most of the input voltage will drop across R1, making VOUT much smaller than VIN.
•	If R2 is much larger than R1, VOUT will approach the value of VIN.
•	If R1 is equal to R2, VOUT will be half of VIN.
Bleeder current refers to the current that flows through the voltage divider resistors. It's called "bleeder" because it continually draws or "bleeds" off a small amount of current, regardless of whether there's any connected load to the VOUT of the divider.
Power dissipated in a voltage divider = VIN2/(R1+R2)
Uses of a voltage divider
1.	Potential Measurement: Often, one might need to measure a voltage that's too high for a given instrument. Using a voltage divider, the voltage can be scaled down to a level that's safe and readable.
2.	Biasing Transistors: In transistor amplifier circuits, voltage dividers are employed to set the base voltage, ensuring the transistor operates in its desired region.
